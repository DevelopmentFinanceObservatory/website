## How to interpret

The switch above the chart moves between two views. "FDL, 2024 data" shows the groups the [Finance for Development Lab](https://findevlab.org/emerging-debt-challenges-for-developing-countries/) published in March 2026. "DFO, 2025 data" applies the same test to 2025 data, with the DFO's projection of debt service to 2031.

### How FDL sorts countries

FDL applies the IMF and World Bank's debt thresholds to 58 low and lower-middle-income countries. It separates countries that owe too much from countries that owe a manageable amount but cannot keep up with repayments.

1. FDL checks external public and publicly guaranteed debt at present value as a share of GDP in the base year. Above 30%, 40%, or 55% for weak, medium, or strong capacity, it puts the country at risk of insolvency.
2. For the remaining countries, it checks external public debt service as a share of government revenue in the base year and each of the six years after it. Above 14%, 18%, or 23% in any of those years, FDL calls the country illiquid.
3. Countries below both thresholds in every year are at lower risk.

Each threshold depends on a country's debt-carrying capacity (weak, medium, or strong) from its latest IMF–World Bank debt sustainability analysis. The 11 countries without one are assessed under the IMF's framework for countries with market access, which sets no capacity. The page rates them strong, extending the rule FDL's 2023 note applies to IBRD borrowers.

## Notes

Based on 2024 data, this tool puts <FdlMatch data={thresholds.data} /> countries in the same group as FDL.

That means we get different results for four countries.

- FDL places Mozambique at risk of insolvency, here we place it above the debt-service threshold. Its debt sits just below the debt threshold at our estimate of present value.
- FDL places Haiti and Sierra Leone at lower risk, here we place them above the debt-service threshold.
- FDL places Comoros illiquid, here we put it below both.

The differences are likely methodological. FDL has not published the updated model behind its 2026 groups, so we follow the method FDL published in 2023.

### Other methodological notes

- The debt test counts public and publicly guaranteed debt without IMF credit, which matches FDL's 2024 groups most closely. Debt service includes payments to the IMF.
- The IMF–World Bank thresholds are set on the present value of debt, which the World Bank does not publish. This page estimates it by discounting the concessional share of each country's debt by the average grant element of its new loans since 2015.
- Revenue is IMF WEO general government revenue, which includes grants. The IMF–World Bank thresholds use revenue excluding grants, so ratios are lower for countries that rely on aid.
- Debt service after the base year (2026 to 2031 in the DFO view, 2025 to 2030 in FDL's) our projection for this page. Payments already scheduled on existing loans, from the World Bank, plus payments on the new loans each country would need to cover its financing gap, following the method FDL set out in [2023](https://findevlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/FDL_Debt-distress-update_final20231204.pdf). New loans use the creditor type's average terms from 2015 to 2019.
- Like FDL's method, the projection does not subtract disbursements still due on loans already signed. New borrowing, and the debt service on it, may therefore run high, which pushes some countries towards illiquid.
- One year above the debt-service threshold is enough to count as illiquid. This is different from the IMF–World Bank framework, which can discount a one-year breach.
- Pakistan has no projection in either view, and Senegal none in the 2025 view, because the IMF WEO lacks the forecasts the projection needs. Both are sorted on the years with data.
- FDL leaves out 17 low and lower-middle-income countries for lack of data: Afghanistan, Bolivia, Eritrea, Kiribati, Lebanon, Micronesia, Namibia, North Korea, Somalia, South Sudan, Sri Lanka, Sudan, Syria, Timor-Leste, Togo, Yemen, and Zimbabwe.
